Testing a satellite water balance for groundwater recharge in tropical basement catchments

Upper Ouémé, Benin, and Upper Ogun, Nigeria, 2018–2024

David Oluwayinka OloyedeB.Tech, Remote Sensing and GIS, Federal University of Technology, Akure

Summary

Can free satellite data tell us how much rain recharges groundwater where no wells are monitored? In the Upper Ouémé, where the answer can be checked against river flow, the numbers do not add up: — mm of water a year is unaccounted for.

Rainfall (CHIRPS v2.0, CHIRPS v3.0, TAMSAT v3.1) minus evapotranspiration (FAO WaPOR v3) minus river flow should be close to zero over several years. With river flow estimated from the runoff ratio measured at Bétérou in 1988–2010, it leaves —% of rainfall in every year of 2018–2024 and with every product.

That water cannot be stored underground; it would need the water table to rise at least — m. A higher runoff ratio, of —, would close the gap, but no recent flow record exists to test it.

The gap is as large as recharge measured in wells in Benin (about 4% and 5–24% of rainfall), so without recent river and well records a satellite balance cannot measure recharge here. The Upper Ogun in Nigeria, with no flow record at all, can be described but not tested.

1Why test it

Rural water in Benin and south-west Nigeria comes largely from weathered crystalline rock, aquifers that across tropical Africa hold little water but are refilled often (MacDonald et al., 2021). Wells in Benin show recharge of about 4% of rainfall (Kotchoni et al., 2019) and 5–24% in the Donga sub-basin of the Upper Ouémé (Kamagaté et al., 2007), but such records are rare. Satellites measure rainfall and evapotranspiration everywhere, so subtracting river flow should leave what recharged the aquifer. Each of those terms, though, has errors of 20–30% (Blatchford et al., 2020; Ogbu et al., 2020), as large as recharge itself. The approach has to be tested where an independent check exists before it is used where none does (Scanlon et al., 2002).

This note asks: does the balance close at Bétérou; if not, what is the leftover water; and what can the same method say about the Upper Ogun?

2Study area

The Upper Ouémé (— km²) drains to the Bétérou gauge in central Benin. It lies on crystalline basement with one rainy season a year (Kodja et al., 2020); groundwater sits in weathered rock above fissured rock, mostly within 100 m of the surface (Lachassagne et al., 2021). The Upper Ogun (— km²) is the Ogun basin above the Oyan confluence in Nigeria; no recharge study of it was found. Both were traced from HydroBASINS (Lehner & Grill, 2013).

5What it means

The leftover water is not storage, so it is error: most likely river flow that was higher than assumed, or evapotranspiration that WaPOR underestimates. Even a balance that closed would show net recharge, because satellite evapotranspiration includes water roots draw back from the water table, while wells show gross recharge (Kamagaté et al., 2007). And a gap that is a fixed share of rainfall will look like recharge, which also scales with rainfall (MacDonald et al., 2021), without being recharge. In the Upper Ogun the method gives rainfall minus evapotranspiration of — mm, untested.

6Limits

Each row of Table 3 changes one assumption. Seven wet years, rainfall products that share gauges, and an evapotranspiration product not checked on the ground also limit what can be said.

7Conclusion

With the data available, a satellite water balance does not close at Bétérou, and its error is as large as groundwater recharge. It shows where rain exceeds evapotranspiration, not how much reaches the aquifer. Three records would settle it: river flow at Bétérou for 2018–2024, the AMMA-CATCH well levels in the Upper Ouémé (Galle et al., 2018), for which the analysis code is ready, and ground measurements of evapotranspiration (Mamadou et al., 2016).
