The study in plain terms

  • The place. Two river catchments on hard basement rock, where most villages drink from shallow wells: the Upper Ouémé in Benin and the Upper Ogun in Nigeria, about 10,000 km² each.
  • The question. How much rain soaks down to refill the groundwater ()? Can free satellite data answer that where wells are not monitored?
  • How. Satellite rainfall, minus the water that plants, soil and lakes send back to the air (), checked against how much water the Ouémé river really carries at Bétérou.

      How this was measured

      1. Catchments

      Both catchments are traced upstream through the HydroBASINS level-9 river network (NEXT_DOWN): the Upper Ouémé from the unit containing the Bétérou gauge (9.199°N, 2.268°E; HydroBASINS area 10,143 km², within 0.7% of the published 10,070–10,076 km²), and the Upper Ogun from the unit above the Oyan confluence (9,574 km²). Areas are computed on the WGS84 ellipsoid.

      2. Data

      Rainfall: CHIRPS v2.0 and v3.0 annual totals (0.05°) and TAMSAT v3.1 monthly totals (0.0375°, rfe_filled) summed per year. Evapotranspiration: FAO WaPOR v3 Level 1 actual evapotranspiration and interception (L1-AETI-A, 300 m). WaPOR v3's precipitation layer (L1-PCP-A) matches CHIRPS v3.0 to within 0.25 mm/year in every catchment-year, although its metadata only says "CHIRPS". Every downloaded file's URL, size, access time and SHA-256 are recorded in data/processed/provenance.json.

      3. Catchment averages

      Area-weighted means (cos latitude) of every pixel whose centre lies inside the catchment, accepted only when at least 95% of the area has data (all were 100%). The map shows each 5 km CHIRPS cell, with WaPOR evapotranspiration averaged onto the same cells, so nothing finer than the coarsest input is implied.

      4. The water-balance check

      Published mean flows at Bétérou (47.5 m³/s in 1971–1987 and 54.5 m³/s in 1988–2010; Proc. IAHS 383, 2020) give runoff of 148.8 and 170.8 mm/year, or 0.136 and 0.142 of rainfall. Expected flow in 2018–2024 = 0.142 × rainfall, the higher ratio, which gives the smaller gap. Gap = rainfall − evapotranspiration − expected flow. Over several years, changes in stored water are small, so the gap should be close to zero.

      5. Could the gap be stored groundwater?

      Storing a depth of water S in an aquifer whose specific yield is Sy raises the water table by S / Sy. Weathered crystalline aquifers have Sy of about 0.2–1.5% (Lachassagne, Dewandel & Wyns, 2021). The gap is also compared with recharge estimated from groundwater levels in Benin basement: about 4% of rainfall (Kotchoni et al., 2019, 19–25-year records) and 5–24% (Kamagaté et al., 2007, two years in the Donga sub-basin of the Upper Ouémé).

      6. Wet and dry years

      Each year is compared with the terciles of 1991–2020, separately for CHIRPS v2.0 and v3.0: "dry" below the lower third, "wet" above the upper third.

      7. Known limitations

      No measured river flow for 2018–2024 was available, so the 1988–2010 runoff ratio is assumed to still apply. No river-flow data were found for the Upper Ogun. WaPOR v3 has not been checked against ground measurements in these catchments (WaPOR v2 errors across Africa were 29–31%; Blatchford et al., 2020). The rainfall products all rely on sparse rain gauges. 2018–2024 was wetter than 1991–2020, so drought years are not represented. A satellite balance sees net recharge (after groundwater taken up by vegetation), while wells see gross recharge. Validation against AMMA-CATCH well water levels is pending data access; the code is ready in scripts/04_validate_wtf.py.
      About this atlas

      How to use this site

      This atlas tests whether free satellite data can estimate how much rain recharges groundwater in crystalline basement rock, using the Upper Ouémé in Benin, where river flow has been measured, and applies the same steps to the Upper Ogun in Nigeria. Every number is computed by the repository's own scripts.

      Using the site

      1. Overview: the short answer, headline numbers and findings, each with a verdict.
      2. Map: switch layers at the top left. Each square is one 5 km satellite cell; hover it to read the value. Click a catchment to open it.
      3. Catchment: where the rain goes, as one bar, plus the key numbers.
      4. Year by year: four charts and a table for 2018–2024. Hover a chart to read the same year in all four.
      5. Is the gap real?: why the gap cannot be stored water, and what would close it.
      6. Rainfall estimate (below the catchment chips) switches every number between the three satellite rainfall products.
      7. Share copies a link to exactly this view; Data downloads the numbers or a map image.
