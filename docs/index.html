The Missing Millimetres

I wanted to know how much of the rain that falls on two West African rivers soaks down to refill the groundwater people drink from, using free satellite data. The numbers refused to add up. This page is about why, and about what that tells us.

01 · The place

Two rivers, one kind of rock

The Upper Ouémé in Benin and the Upper Ogun in Nigeria each drain about 10,000 km². Both sit on old crystalline basement: granite and gneiss that hold water only in a weathered layer near the surface.

Across much of Benin and south-west Nigeria, rural water comes from shallow wells dug into that layer. How fast it refills decides how much water a well can safely give. It is rarely measured.

02 · The shortcut

Satellites see what wells can't

Long records of well water levels exist in only a few places. Satellites see every square kilometre, every year. So the shortcut is tempting: take the rain a satellite measures, subtract what goes back to the air, and call the rest the water available for rivers and groundwater.

09 · Why it matters

As big as the thing I was trying to measure

So how big is — mm? Researchers who tracked well water levels in Benin's basement found recharge of about 4% of rainfall over 19–25 years (Kotchoni et al., 2019), and 5–24% in the Donga, a small catchment inside the Upper Ouémé (Kamagaté et al., 2007). My gap is —% of rainfall.

The error in the method is as large as the signal I wanted. A balance that is off by this much cannot measure recharge, however carefully it is computed.

12 · What I'm taking away

The gap is the finding

I set out to produce a recharge number. I am ending with something I trust more: a measured limit on what satellite data can tell us here.

01

Rain minus ET is not recharge

In the one catchment where it can be checked, the satellite balance is off by — mm a year, every year.

02

The error is the size of the signal

The gap is —% of rainfall; published recharge from wells is 4% and 5–24%. The method cannot separate the two.

03

Field data is the missing piece

River flow, well water levels and ground measurements of evapotranspiration would show which input is wrong.

What would change my mind

  1. Well water levels from the AMMA-CATCH observatoryAbout 20 piezometers and 28 wells have been monitored in the Upper Ouémé since 1999 (Galle et al., 2018). The code to analyse them is written and tested; I am waiting for data access.
  2. River flow at Bétérou for 2018–2024It would show directly whether the river now carries more of the rain than it did in 1988–2010.
  3. Ground measurements of evapotranspirationTwo flux towers ran in northern Benin in 2008–2010, one of them inside this catchment (Mamadou et al., 2016), before WaPOR v3 begins. Newer measurements would test the biggest number in the budget.

13 · Lab notes

How the study changed as I worked

Research is rarely a straight line. These are the turns this one took, kept here because each of them changed a number on this page.

  1. The first rainfall check failed

    WaPOR's rainfall did not match CHIRPS v2.0: they were up to — mm apart in a single year. Digging in, I found WaPOR v3 uses CHIRPS v3.0. Instead of hiding the mismatch, I added v3.0 as a third rainfall estimate.

  2. I dropped the neat answer

    My plan was to box recharge in between two numbers. Once the balance failed to close, those bounds would mostly have measured error, so I took them out and reported the gap itself.

  3. I checked the obvious escape route

    Before calling the gap an error, I worked out how far the water table would have to rise to store it. Tens of metres at the very least, which ruled it out.

  4. I found a study from inside the catchment

    At first I compared the gap with a single published recharge figure, 4% of rainfall. Then I found work from the Donga, inside the Upper Ouémé, reporting 5–24%. I rewrote the comparison to show both, and the conclusion got more careful: the gap is as large as recharge, not "two to three times" it.

  5. I caught my own mistake

    When I built the "close the gap" sliders, I noticed my estimate of how much rainfall would need to drop ignored that the river's share falls with it. The corrected figure is —% lower rainfall, a little more than I first wrote.

  6. I stress-tested the river's share

    The share 0.142 comes from rainfall published with the flow record. Recomputing it from each CHIRPS version's own 1988–2010 rainfall made the gap slightly bigger (— and — mm), so the numbers on this page are, if anything, cautious.

How it was measured

Catchments

Traced upstream through the HydroBASINS level-9 river network: the Upper Ouémé from the unit containing the Bétérou gauge (9.199°N, 2.268°E; 10,143 km², about 0.7% above the published 10,070–10,076 km²), the Upper Ogun from the unit above the Oyan confluence (9,574 km²).

Rain and evapotranspiration

CHIRPS v2.0 and v3.0 annual rainfall (0.05°), TAMSAT v3.1 monthly rainfall summed per year, and FAO WaPOR v3 actual evapotranspiration and interception (300 m), 2018–2024. Catchment values are area-weighted means of every pixel whose centre lies inside; all had complete data. On the map, CHIRPS v3.0, TAMSAT and WaPOR are averaged onto the 5 km CHIRPS v2.0 cells, so nothing finer than the rainfall data is implied.

The river check

Published mean flows at Bétérou (47.5 m³/s in 1971–1987, 54.5 m³/s in 1988–2010) equal 148.8 and 170.8 mm of runoff a year, 0.136 and 0.142 of the rainfall reported for those periods. Expected flow for 2018–2024 uses the higher share, which gives the smaller gap. Gap = rainfall − evapotranspiration − expected flow.

Could the gap be stored?

Water-table rise = stored water ÷ specific yield, with specific yield 0.2–1.5% for weathered crystalline aquifers (Lachassagne, Dewandel & Wyns, 2021). Published recharge for comparison: about 4% of rainfall (Kotchoni et al., 2019) and 5–24% (Kamagaté et al., 2007).

Limits

No measured river flow for 2018–2024 was available, so the 1988–2010 share is assumed to still apply. No flow data were found for the Upper Ogun. WaPOR v3 has not been checked against ground measurements here. All rainfall products rely on sparse rain gauges. 2018–2024 was wetter than 1991–2020, so no drought years are included. Validation against AMMA-CATCH wells is pending data access.
