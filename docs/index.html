Abstract

Groundwater in the weathered crystalline basement of Benin and south-west Nigeria supplies much of the rural water, but recharge to these aquifers is poorly quantified and few wells are monitored. Satellite rainfall and evapotranspiration cover every catchment, which makes a water balance an attractive way to estimate recharge where wells are absent. This study tests that approach in the Upper Ouémé at Bétérou (Benin, — km²), where historical river flow is published, and applies it unchanged to the Upper Ogun (Nigeria, — km²) for 2018–2024. Rainfall comes from CHIRPS v2.0, CHIRPS v3.0 and TAMSAT v3.1, and actual evapotranspiration from FAO WaPOR v3. No discharge is available for 2018–2024, so river flow was estimated from the runoff ratio observed at Bétérou in 1988–2010. With that ratio, the Upper Ouémé balance leaves a residual of — mm per year (—% of rainfall), positive in every year and with every rainfall product. The residual cannot be water stored in the aquifer: holding it would need a water-table rise of at least — m. It is, however, conditional on the transferred runoff ratio. A ratio of —, inside the single-year range reported for a sub-basin of the same catchment, closes the balance. The residual is as large as recharge estimated from groundwater levels in Benin basement, about 4% and 5–24% of rainfall. With current satellite products, and without contemporary discharge and well records, a catchment water balance cannot resolve recharge here.

1Introduction

Across much of tropical Africa, groundwater is held in weathered crystalline rock. A synthesis of 134 ground-based estimates found these aquifers to combine low storage with high, regular recharge, and found long-term rainfall to be the only factor related to long-term recharge at continental scale (MacDonald et al., 2021). In Benin, groundwater-level records give recharge of about 4% of rainfall in a weathered-crystalline aquifer over 19–25 years (Kotchoni et al., 2019) and 5–24% over two years in the Donga sub-basin of the Upper Ouémé (Kamagaté et al., 2007). Such records exist at few sites.

Recharge methods differ in the time and space scales they represent, and estimates from more than one method are needed before any one is trusted (Scanlon et al., 2002). The water-table fluctuation method needs well records and a specific yield (Healy & Cook, 2002). Where neither exists, a catchment water balance is an alternative: rainfall minus evapotranspiration minus river flow leaves the water that recharged the aquifer or changed storage. Satellite products now provide the first two terms everywhere.

Each term carries an error that may be as large as the recharge it is meant to reveal. WaPOR v2 evapotranspiration differed from flux-tower measurements across Africa by 29–31% (Blatchford et al., 2020), rainfall products over Nigeria had biases of 20–26% (Ogbu et al., 2020), and river flow is rarely measured in the same years as the satellite record. Whether a satellite balance is precise enough for recharge therefore has to be tested where an independent check exists, before it is applied where none does.

This study makes that test in the Upper Ouémé, where river flow at Bétérou has been published, and asks three questions:

  1. RQ1. Does a satellite water balance close against the river flow observed at Bétérou?
  2. RQ2. What does the residual represent, and how sensitive is it to the rainfall product, the evapotranspiration estimate and the runoff assumption?
  3. RQ3. What can the same method, applied unchanged, say about the ungauged Upper Ogun?

2Study area

The Upper Ouémé is the part of the Ouémé basin upstream of the Bétérou gauge in central Benin. The northern Ouémé lies on the Dahomeyan crystalline basement and has a Sudanian climate with a single rainy season at Bétérou (Kodja et al., 2020). In such terrain, groundwater is held mainly in a weathered layer (saprolite) above fissured rock, mostly within the upper 100 m (Lachassagne et al., 2021). In the Donga sub-basin (586 km²), inside the catchment, recharge is by direct infiltration of rainfall, the weathered layer is up to a few tens of metres thick, and borehole abstraction is a small share of recharge (Kamagaté et al., 2007). Rainfall in 2018–2024 averaged — mm per year (CHIRPS v2.0).

The Upper Ogun is the Ogun basin in south-west Nigeria upstream of its confluence with the Oyan River. It averaged — mm of rainfall per year in 2018–2024. No published recharge estimate or hydrogeological study specific to this catchment was found. It lies north of the coastal sedimentary basin, and the weathering-profile description above is assumed, not shown, to apply.

3Data and method

The rainfall products are not independent. CHIRPS v2.0 and v3.0 share a method, WaPOR's precipitation layer is CHIRPS v3.0 (Section 4.1), and all three products are adjusted to rain gauges, of which there are few. Their spread is therefore a lower bound on rainfall uncertainty, not a measure of it. No gauge record for 2018–2024 was available, so agreement between products is reported, not error.

3.1Catchment averages

Annual totals were averaged over each catchment, weighting pixels by area and including every pixel whose centre lies inside the boundary. A catchment-year was accepted only if at least 95% of its area had valid data; every catchment-year did. WaPOR's 300 m evapotranspiration was averaged onto the 5 km rainfall grid for the maps. Each year was classed wet, normal or dry against the terciles of 1991–2020 CHIRPS rainfall.

5Discussion

5.1What the residual contains

Over several years the residual equals the change in soil and groundwater storage, plus groundwater leaving the catchment underground, plus abstraction, plus the errors in rainfall, evapotranspiration and the estimated river flow. Storage is ruled out (Section 4.4). Underground outflow is assumed small in these shallow aquifers, and abstraction in the Donga sub-basin is a small share of recharge (Kamagaté et al., 2007); neither was measured here. What remains is error, and the largest single candidate is the river flow: it was not measured, and the ratio that closes the balance is within the range observed in single years nearby. A higher runoff ratio in 2018–2024 than in 1988–2010 is plausible, because the period was wetter and runoff ratios tend to rise in wet years, but it has not been shown. Evapotranspiration is the second candidate. WaPOR v3 has not been evaluated in these catchments, and the evaluation of v2 found errors larger than the change needed here.

5.2Net and gross recharge

Satellite evapotranspiration includes water drawn from the water table by deep roots and wet valley bottoms. Even a closed satellite balance would therefore measure net recharge, while the water-table fluctuation method measures gross recharge. In the Donga sub-basin, recharge of 5–24% of rainfall coexisted with a weak groundwater contribution to river flow (Kamagaté et al., 2007), which is consistent with much of the recharge returning to the atmosphere. A satellite balance and a well-based estimate would not be expected to agree even if both were exact.

5.3Relation to other estimates

That the residual is of the same magnitude as published recharge is not evidence that it is recharge. Across Africa, long-term recharge scales with long-term rainfall (MacDonald et al., 2021), and so does every error that is a fixed share of rainfall, including the error of a transferred runoff ratio. A residual of —% of rainfall is what an unmeasured flow term of that size would produce whether or not any of it recharged the aquifer.

5.4Implications

For basement catchments of this size, the uncertainty of a satellite water balance is as large as recharge. Such a balance can show where rainfall exceeds evapotranspiration and how products disagree, but it cannot estimate recharge without measured river flow for the same years and an independent check from wells. The order of the work matters: the balance should first be closed against measured flow, and only then interpreted.

6Limitations and future work

Table 4 summarises how much each source of uncertainty that could be quantified changes the mean residual. The ranges are the results of alternative assumptions, not statistical confidence intervals.

  • No measured river flow for 2018–2024 was available. The runoff ratio of 1988–2010 is assumed to apply, and changes in land cover or abstraction since 2010 were not assessed.
  • No river-flow data were found for the Upper Ogun, so its balance cannot be tested, and the two catchments can be compared only in rainfall and evapotranspiration.
  • WaPOR v3 has not been evaluated against ground measurements in these catchments; the cited accuracy is for v2.
  • The rainfall products are not independent, and no gauge record for 2018–2024 was available to measure their error.
  • The specific-yield range is a regional value from the literature, not a measurement in these catchments.
  • 2018–2024 was wetter than 1991–2020, and seven years are too few to describe long-term behaviour.
  • The published recharge values come from other periods and, for Kotchoni et al. (2019), other sites.
  • The hydrogeological description of the Upper Ogun is assumed from the regional setting.

Three data sets would resolve the residual. None has been analysed here.

  1. River discharge at Bétérou for 2018–2024, which would replace the assumed runoff ratio and show whether the balance closes.
  2. Groundwater levels from the AMMA-CATCH observatory, about 20 piezometers and 28 wells monitored in the Upper Ouémé since 1999 (Galle et al., 2018). The analysis code is written and tested on synthetic data only (scripts/04_validate_wtf.py); access requires registration.
  3. Ground measurements of evapotranspiration. Eddy-covariance stations ran in northern Benin in 2008–2010, one inside this catchment (Mamadou et al., 2016), before the WaPOR v3 record begins.

7Conclusion

RQ1. With the runoff ratio observed in 1988–2010, a satellite water balance does not close at Bétérou in 2018–2024: — mm per year are unaccounted for, in every year and with every product. Because river flow was not measured in those years, this shows that the balance cannot be closed with the data available, not that the satellite products are wrong.

RQ2. The residual is not stored groundwater. It is error in one or more terms, most plausibly the estimated river flow or the evapotranspiration, and it is as large as published recharge. A runoff ratio within the range observed in single years nearby removes it.

RQ3. In the Upper Ogun the method gives rainfall minus evapotranspiration only. The products disagree there by about twice as much as in the Upper Ouémé, and nothing can be said about recharge.

The next step is measured discharge at Bétérou for the satellite years, followed by the AMMA-CATCH well records. Until then, satellite water balances in these catchments describe the surplus of rainfall over evapotranspiration, not recharge.

Methodological changes during the study

  1. The WaPOR v3 precipitation layer did not match CHIRPS v2.0, with differences of up to — mm in a catchment-year. It was found to equal CHIRPS v3.0, which was then added as a third rainfall estimate.
  2. Bounds on net recharge were planned but dropped: with a residual this large, they would mostly reflect error. The residual is reported instead.
  3. The comparison with published recharge was extended from one value (4%) to include the Donga sub-basin study (5–24%), which narrowed the conclusion from "two to three times recharge" to "as large as recharge".
  4. The rainfall change needed to close the balance was corrected to account for river flow falling with rainfall, from 9.7–13.0% to 11.3–15.1% (version 0.2).
  5. The runoff ratio was checked against each CHIRPS version's own 1988–2010 rainfall (version 0.2), and against TAMSAT's (version 0.3).
  6. River flow is now converted to a depth over the same catchment polygon as the other terms, instead of the published 10,072 km² (version 0.3).
  7. The residual's dependence on the runoff ratio became a main result, and the conclusion was reworded: the balance cannot be closed with the data available, which is not proof that the satellite products are wrong (version 0.3).

Author's note

I started this study to estimate how much rain recharges the basement aquifers that rural wells in Benin and south-west Nigeria depend on. The water balance did not close, and working out why became the study. I will rerun the analysis when recent Bétérou discharge and the AMMA-CATCH well records are available.
