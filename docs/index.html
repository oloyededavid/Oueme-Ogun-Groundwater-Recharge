Abstract

Groundwater in the weathered crystalline basement of Benin and south-west Nigeria supplies much of the rural water, yet recharge to these aquifers is poorly quantified. This study tests whether a satellite water balance can estimate it. Rainfall comes from CHIRPS v2.0, CHIRPS v3.0 and TAMSAT v3.1, and actual evapotranspiration from FAO WaPOR v3. The test catchment is the Upper Ouémé at Bétérou (Benin, — km²), where river flow has been measured; the same method is applied to the Upper Ogun (Nigeria, — km²). For 2018–2024, rainfall minus evapotranspiration in the Upper Ouémé exceeds the river flow expected from the observed runoff ratio by — mm per year (—% of rainfall), in every year and with every rainfall product. Storing this residual would require the water table to rise by at least — m, so it is not stored water but error in the rainfall, evapotranspiration or river-flow terms. It is as large as recharge reported from groundwater-level studies in Benin basement, about 4% and 5–24% of rainfall. At this scale, a satellite water balance cannot resolve recharge without field data.

1Study area

Both catchments lie on Precambrian crystalline basement. Groundwater is held mainly in a weathered layer (saprolite) above fissured rock, mostly within the upper 100 m (Lachassagne et al., 2021). The Upper Ouémé is defined by the river gauge at Bétérou, the Upper Ogun by the confluence with the Oyan River. Both were traced upstream through the HydroBASINS level-9 network (Lehner & Grill, 2013). The HydroBASINS area at Bétérou, — km², is about 0.7% larger than the published 10,070–10,076 km².

2Data and method

2.1Catchment averages

Annual totals were averaged over each catchment, weighting pixels by area and including every pixel whose centre lies inside the boundary. Every catchment-year had complete data. Each year was classed wet, normal or dry against the terciles of 1991–2020.

2.2Water balance

Over several years, changes in stored water are small, so rainfall P, actual evapotranspiration ET and river flow Q should leave a residual ε close to zero (Eq. 2). No river flow was available for 2018–2024, so it was estimated from the published mean flows at Bétérou: 47.5 m³/s in 1971–1987 and 54.5 m³/s in 1988–2010 (Kodja et al., 2020). These equal — and — of the rainfall reported for those periods. The higher runoff ratio, r = —, was used because it gives the smaller residual (Eq. 1). Stored as groundwater, the residual would raise the water table by Δh, where Sy is the specific yield (Eq. 3).

Q = r P(1)
ε = P − ET − Q(2)
Δh = Σε / Sy(3)

2.3Tests of the residual

  • Storage. The rise Δh (Eq. 3) needed to store the 2018–2024 residual, with Sy of 0.2–1.5% for weathered crystalline aquifers (Lachassagne et al., 2021).
  • Scale. Comparison with recharge estimated from groundwater levels in Benin basement: about 4% of rainfall from 19–25-year records (Kotchoni et al., 2019) and 5–24% from two years in the Donga sub-basin of the Upper Ouémé (Kamagaté et al., 2007).
  • Sensitivity. The change in ET, P or r that sets the mean residual to zero, one term at a time. Lowering P also lowers Q (Eq. 1).
  • Ratio robustness. The runoff ratio recomputed from each CHIRPS version's own 1988–2010 rainfall.

4Discussion

4.1What the residual contains

Over several years the residual equals the change in soil and groundwater storage, plus groundwater leaving the catchment underground, plus abstraction, plus errors in rainfall, evapotranspiration and river flow. Storage is ruled out (Section 3.2). Underground outflow is assumed small in these shallow aquifers, and abstraction in the Donga sub-basin is a small share of recharge (Kamagaté et al., 2007). Most of the residual must therefore be error in the inputs, or a change in the runoff ratio since 1988–2010.

4.2Net and gross recharge

Satellite evapotranspiration includes water drawn from the water table by deep roots and wet valley bottoms. Even a closed satellite balance therefore measures net recharge, while the water-table fluctuation method measures gross recharge. In the Donga sub-basin, recharge of 5–24% of rainfall coexisted with a weak groundwater contribution to river flow (Kamagaté et al., 2007). This is consistent with much of the recharge returning to the atmosphere.

4.3Implications

At catchment scale and with current products, the uncertainty of a satellite water balance is as large as recharge in basement aquifers. Such balances can show where water is in surplus and how products disagree, but recharge estimates need river-flow records and well observations.

5Limitations and next steps

  • No measured river flow for 2018–2024 was available; the 1988–2010 runoff ratio is assumed to still apply.
  • No river-flow data were found for the Upper Ogun, so its balance cannot be tested.
  • WaPOR v3 has not been evaluated against ground measurements in these catchments.
  • All rainfall products rely on sparse rain gauges and are not fully independent.
  • 2018–2024 was wetter than 1991–2020, so drought years are not represented.
  • The published recharge values come from other periods and, for Kotchoni et al. (2019), other sites.

Three data sets would resolve the residual:

  1. Groundwater levels from the AMMA-CATCH observatory, about 20 piezometers and 28 wells monitored in the Upper Ouémé since 1999 (Galle et al., 2018). The analysis code is ready (scripts/04_validate_wtf.py); data access requires registration.
  2. River discharge at Bétérou for 2018–2024, which would replace the assumed runoff ratio.
  3. Ground measurements of evapotranspiration. Eddy-covariance stations ran in northern Benin in 2008–2010, one inside this catchment (Mamadou et al., 2016), before the WaPOR v3 record begins.

Methodological changes during the study

  1. The WaPOR v3 precipitation layer did not match CHIRPS v2.0, with differences of up to — mm in a catchment-year. It was found to equal CHIRPS v3.0, which was then added as a third rainfall estimate.
  2. Bounds on net recharge were planned but dropped: with a residual this large, they would mostly reflect error. The residual is reported instead.
  3. The comparison with published recharge was extended from one value (4%) to include the Donga sub-basin study (5–24%), which narrowed the conclusion from "two to three times recharge" to "as large as recharge".
  4. The rainfall change needed to close the balance was corrected to account for river flow falling with rainfall, from 9.7–13.0% to —% (version 0.2).
  5. A robustness check of the runoff ratio against each CHIRPS version's own 1988–2010 rainfall was added (version 0.2).

Author's note

I started this study to estimate how much rain recharges the basement aquifers that rural wells in Benin and south-west Nigeria depend on. The water balance did not close, and the size of that failure became the main result. I will rerun the analysis when the AMMA-CATCH well records and recent Bétérou discharge are available.
